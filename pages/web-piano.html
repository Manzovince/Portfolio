Octave

Octave started as a small personal project: to understand how sounds are made, to learn the piano, and to learn web programming along the way. It grew a lot since then, from code written by hand to several rounds of improvements made with AI. Today it is a piano in the browser, with a synthesizer, a mode to learn songs and chords, and a mode to make your own sounds.

Play Octave

The very first idea was simple: could the keys I play light up a strip of LEDs? I had never used an Arduino, and I barely knew how MIDI worked. The first version was one octave and a sine wave on the screen.

It grew into a web piano that named chords and lit an LED strip. On the side, I built Phase, a synthesizer for my ROLI LUMI keyboards. Octave brings the two together, rebuilt from scratch around one question: what should the screen show while you play?

Play

As you play, notes rise from the keys. The chord is named above, the way a musician would write it.

You can split the keyboard in two, with a different sound for each hand. There is also an arpeggiator, a step sequencer, and a view that writes what you play as sheet music, to download as an image or a MIDI file.

Learn

Open a song, from the examples or your own MIDI file, and its notes fall onto the keys, one colour per hand. Pick a hand: Octave plays the other one and waits for you on each chord.

Exercises teach chords and scales, and light up the keys to play, on screen and on the LUMI.

Create

The sound engine comes from Phase and works like the famous Yamaha DX7. It comes with thirty-five original sounds, from electric pianos to bells, and it can open and save DX7 sound banks.

The editor shows how the sound is built, and animates it as you play, so you can see why a sound is bright or soft.

Made for fingers

Everything is made for touch: big buttons, easy sliders, and a keyboard always at the bottom of the screen that you can play with several fingers. On a phone, the panels fold under the keyboard; on a computer, they sit beside it.

What I learned

Merging two apps was about choosing what each screen is for, not adding features. Three modes, a keyboard always in the same place, and all the settings in one panel.

Octave works best in Chrome or Edge, which support MIDI keyboards. On an iPhone or iPad, use the Web MIDI Browser app to play with a keyboard. Without one, the on-screen keys and the computer keyboard work everywhere.
